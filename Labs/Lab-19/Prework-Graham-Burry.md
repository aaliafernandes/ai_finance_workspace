## Reading notes

### Benjamin Graham

Graham's framework starts with protecting principal through a margin of
safety, rather than relying on an optimistic growth story. He treats market
prices as potentially emotional ("Mr. Market") and emphasizes measurable
tests: financial condition, earnings and dividend history, moderate P/E and
P/B ratios, net current asset value (NCAV), and earnings-power value (EPV).
The practical question for NVDA would be whether a conservative, no-growth
value estimate leaves enough discount to the market price; a popular,
high-multiple company would need particularly careful scrutiny under this
approach.

### Michael Burry

Burry's framework is forensic and contrarian. It asks whether reported
revenue and earnings are supported by cash flow, then looks for warning signs
in receivables, accruals, capitalization policies, stock-based compensation,
debt, leases, goodwill, and SEC-filing footnotes. It also requires constructing
a concrete bear case - even for a company with a strong consensus narrative - and
naming the possible trigger, downside, and evidence that would disprove the
bear case. For NVDA, this means separating reported growth from cash
conversion and testing what happens if demand, margins, or valuation multiples
fall.
